# Evidence and Customer Research

Use this reference before creating personas, offers, proof, FAQs, or customer-language copy.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer sources in this order while preserving source type and date:

1. Direct observation of the current operation and conversion path
2. Current customer, lost-lead, and former-customer interviews
3. Current inquiry messages, call notes, form submissions, complaints, refunds, and support records
4. Current job, capacity, margin, and lead-quality records
5. Authentic reviews with known source and context
6. Search Console, analytics, ad-search terms, and local keyword data
7. Competitor pages and local community discussions
8. Industry research
9. Owner or team assumptions
10. Generated hypotheses

Lower-ranked evidence can identify questions and hypotheses. It does not become a client fact by repetition.

## Research brief

Record:

- Decision this research must inform
- Segment and geography
- Behavior or journey under study
- Known facts
- Unknowns
- Recruiting criteria
- Methods and source access
- Owner
- Dates
- Consent and privacy handling
- Synthesis method
- Decision deadline

Do not recruit only enthusiastic current customers when the question concerns objections, abandoned inquiries, or switching. Include lost leads, former customers, and recent buyers where appropriate.

## Interview approach

Ask open, neutral questions about a recent real event. Follow the sequence rather than forcing every question.

### Trigger and context

- Tell me about the last time you decided you needed help with this.
- What was happening in your home, property, business, or schedule?
- Why did it matter then rather than later?

### Alternatives and search

- What did you try first?
- Where did you look?
- Which options did you compare?
- What words did you use when you searched or asked someone?

### Risk and decision

- What made hiring someone feel difficult or risky?
- What information did you look for before contacting anyone?
- What nearly stopped you?
- What made one option feel safer or more suitable than another?

### Contact and handoff

- How did you prefer to contact them, and why?
- What did you expect to happen after reaching out?
- What actually happened?
- At what point did you feel confident or uncertain?

### Outcome

- What did success make possible for you?
- What was more important than you expected?
- What would you change about the experience?

Avoid:

- “Would you buy this?” future hypotheticals
- “Do you value reliability?” leading abstractions
- Requests for taglines
- Asking participants to design the solution
- Treating politeness as demand

## Evidence ledger

Capture every meaningful item separately:

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Statement or exact phrase | Atomic claim or verbatim customer language |
| Segment | Who the source represents |
| Trigger | What caused action now |
| Job | What they were trying to make possible |
| Pain or fear | Risk, friction, or undesired outcome |
| Desired outcome | Functional or emotional progress |
| Objection | What prevented or delayed action |
| Proof required | What would reduce uncertainty |
| Source | Interview, record, review, analytics, desk research, or assumption |
| Date | When the evidence was created or observed |
| Occurrences | Independent sources, not repeated mentions by one source |
| Confidence | Low, medium, or high with reason |
| Evidence class | Fact, direct customer evidence, hypothesis, or unknown |
| Publication status | Allowed, conditional, hold, or private |

Do not expose personal information in the ledger used for copy. Use participant IDs and store consent separately.

## Synthesis

1. Separate segments before grouping language.
2. Cluster by trigger, job, pain, gain, objection, proof, and preferred next action.
3. Preserve contradictions; do not average them away.
4. Count independent sources, not rhetorical intensity.
5. Identify the smallest segment whose decision process is coherent.
6. Map each proposed message to evidence.
7. Mark unsupported but plausible ideas as hypotheses.
8. Record what evidence would change the decision.

## Desk research

Desk research can reveal market language and failure modes. It cannot prove the client's strengths.

Useful sources include:

- Local review themes across several providers
- Negative reviews and complaint patterns
- Local community questions
- Search queries and autocomplete patterns
- Competitor offer, scope, form, and proof structures
- Licensing or regulatory sources where applicable
- Search Console and actual ad-search terms

Label each finding as:

- Market observation
- Competitor claim
- Customer-language hypothesis
- Compliance requirement
- Client fact only when separately verified

Never copy competitor testimonials, proprietary phrasing, photos, guarantees, or unsupported claims.

## Research sufficiency

Research is sufficient for a provisional page direction when:

- The segment and triggering situation are distinguishable
- The real next action is known
- The most important risks and questions are visible
- The offer can be bounded
- Every public claim has support or a hold label
- The remaining unknowns have owners and do not make the CTA misleading

Research is not sufficient for a proof-heavy final page merely because several plausible messages can be written.
